# Preserving the Information Foundation of National Security

**Draft proposed written testimony for Lieutenant General Paul T. Stanton, USA — not an official or delivered statement**

**Editorial research cutoff: October 7, 2026**

Chair, Ranking Member, and Members of the Committee:

## Opening

Our forces depend on information they can trust: technical records, observations, maps, maintenance histories, intelligence, and the data used to train and evaluate decision-support systems.[^S001][^S003] If a source record is lost, its origin becomes uncertain, or an altered copy is mistaken for the original, later users may be unable to distinguish what was observed from what was generated or changed.[^R014][^R016] This is a readiness concern, not a prediction that the internet or artificial intelligence (AI) will inevitably fail.[^S001][^S003][^R001][^R003][^R011][^R013]

I recommend that Congress establish a focused, defense-led Strategic Information Command within the Department of Defense. Its mission should be to identify, preserve, and make responsibly usable defense-critical information collections, then demonstrate that they can be restored and evaluated when needed. It should not replace the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), collect the entire civilian internet, or claim authority over civilian information. This testimony recommends a new command; it does not present one as existing or as an approved departmental position. I thank the Committee for considering a bounded way to protect a resource on which military readiness increasingly depends.[^S001][^S003]

## The Risk to the Information Base

Consider a simple sequence. Researchers and operators create observations: a sensor reading, a laboratory result, a maintenance report, or a record of what happened during an operation. People and machines can then use those records to produce summaries, translations, synthetic examples, and other derivatives. A later training process may use some of those generated outputs as data. If that process repeatedly replaces or dilutes original observations with generated material, and if its sampling or selection omits unusual patterns or carries forward errors, later models can lose coverage of some features of the source distribution. Researchers call some forms of degradation across generations of model training **model collapse**. It is a studied mechanism under defined conditions, not a proven fate for every model or every online collection.[^R001][^R003]

The distinctions matter. A fixed model does not change its learned parameters simply because web pages change. Retraining on new data can change those parameters; a deployed system can also give worse answers if it retrieves poor external material even though its model weights are unchanged. Deliberate training-data poisoning is a separate threat: an adversary manipulates training material to influence a learned model. Attacks on retrieved context can separately influence deployed answers without retraining. Recursive degradation, by contrast, does not require an attacker. The risks call for careful preservation and evaluation, not for treating every generated item as hostile or every model output as a training record.[^R015][^R001]

The experimental record is mixed and conditional. In one influential study, researchers demonstrated defects across specified recursive-training settings, including an experiment fine-tuning a relatively small language model on a text dataset; the authors did not study a population of deployed frontier models. A later correction changed a theoretical condition, not the article’s empirical results. Other work finds that accumulating original examples can bound some error growth in studied settings, while still observing losses in diversity. Results also depend on whether data are replaced, accumulated, subsampled, curated, verified, and matched to a particular task and model. Some synthetic data improves performance in defined tasks; other tested self-training methods did not improve downstream results. Checking generated examples can help in tested settings, but the checker’s own bias can limit or reverse improvements.[^R009] There is no universal synthetic-data proportion or contamination threshold established by this evidence.[^R001][^R002][^R003][^R040][^R008][^R056]

Online prevalence measures should not be confused with accuracy or training contamination. Pew Research Center reported that 10% of its random sample of 10,000 English-language pages from the July 2026 Common Crawl snapshot showed significant detector-based signs of AI authorship or substantial editing. This was a detector-based estimate for that sample, not a census of all internet content, not a measure of how many pages were inaccurate, and not proof that those pages entered any particular model’s training data. Detector classifications are probabilistic, and the separate post-ChatGPT dated subset was not a random sample of the whole web. Another archive-based study found no statistically significant macro-level decline in factual accuracy under its measures; that observational null result does not prove there is no harm.[^R011][^R012][^R013]

Here, “AI slop” is used only as an informal label for low-quality or unverified generated content, not as a technical synonym for synthetic data. Synthetic examples can be useful, including when they expand coverage or support controlled task training. Human-authored material can also be inaccurate. Collection rules should admit valid, documented instrument observations and algorithmically processed measurements, not just human-authored prose. A creation date before widespread generative AI use does not make a record true, human-authored, or appropriate for a given training purpose. The sensible objective is to preserve independently originated, documented records and observations with their context, not to certify a supposedly pure pre-AI internet.[^R008][^R014][^R016] Preservation should not displace ongoing collection of fresh, mission-relevant observations; historical records also require contextual and quality assessment before training or operational use.[^R016]

## Why This Is a Readiness Issue

Decision support is only as dependable as the information on which it relies, the method by which it was transformed, and the evaluation used to test it. In military settings, unusual cases can matter disproportionately: a rare equipment failure, an uncommon environmental condition, or a low-frequency logistics constraint may be exactly the case a system must not overlook. Retaining original records does not guarantee a model will learn from them. Provenance—the documented origin and history of a record—does not guarantee truth. Preserving records and their provenance can support inspection of what changed. I recommend using those records to evaluate whether a system performs acceptably on cases relevant to its mission.[^R001][^R003][^R009][^R014][^R016]

For example, suppose a deployed unit faces an unexpected engine fault during a movement of critical supplies. A decision-support tool that produces a fluent maintenance recommendation from repeated summaries but cannot trace the underlying observations could fail to surface a rare inspection report documenting a relevant failure mode. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a reported incident or a claim about a particular military system. Keeping the original inspection report, its conditions, and its revisions would not dictate the repair; it would let authorized maintainers and evaluators determine what the report actually says and whether a proposed tool can retrieve and interpret it. The operational objective is dependable human judgment informed by traceable evidence, not automation for its own sake.

This also bears on technological resilience. A change in an online collection alone does not retrain a fixed system, but new training runs and retrieval pipelines can make changing sources operationally relevant. Retaining records, documenting transformations, and testing recovery can help separate data loss, retrieval defects, model-training effects, and intentional manipulation. These controls can help manage those risks; they cannot eliminate all of them. They cannot certify that every preserved statement is true. Preservation should complement testing and accountable human judgment, not replace them.[^R015][^R016][^R017]

This proposal does not depend on predicting how future highly capable or superintelligent systems might use synthetic information. The effects on hypothetical future systems are uncertain; the reviewed evidence does not establish a timeline or measured outcome for such a system.[^R001][^R003][^R009] We need not rely on that forecast. Protecting records needed for present maintenance, logistics, planning, and system evaluation is justified by current readiness needs alone.

## A Focused Strategic Information Command

I recommend establishing a focused military Strategic Information Command within the Department of Defense. I do not propose a new global unified combatant command. Its purpose should be operational accountability for preservation and assurance of defense-critical information, not ownership of all information or a claim that organizational creation itself proves effectiveness. The Secretary of Defense should provide an implementation plan defining internal reporting relationships and resolving overlaps before expansion. The proposal complements, rather than displaces, the work of existing defense and civilian organizations.[^R016][^R017][^R025][^R046]

First, the command should **identify and prioritize** existing defense-critical information collections and their custodians. Priorities should reflect mission dependency, uniqueness, provenance quality, and risk of loss. Examples include original government and scientific observations, authoritative technical and historical records, and lawfully held defense mission datasets. The mission is not indiscriminate capture of the civilian internet. Each collection should have an identified custodian, a stated operational purpose, and documented access and use conditions.

Second, the command should **preserve recoverable originals**. I recommend versioned, access-controlled preservation copies in geographically separated locations, with fixity records (checks of whether stored bytes have changed), chain-of-custody metadata, and restoration tests. Originals should remain distinguishable from cleaned, annotated, translated, summarized, or synthetic derivatives. Documented transformations should make it possible to tell what was altered and by whom when that information is available. Unknown origin must remain unknown; no office should retroactively certify material as “untainted” when its provenance is missing. Preservation should operate within lawful retention schedules and security requirements, not create an unlimited exception to them.[^R014][^R016][^R017]

Third, the command should **enable authorized, traceable use**. It should support records of origin, transformation, rights, and access restrictions, and keep observed material distinct from generated material. Signatures, watermarks, detectors, or checksums can each answer limited questions but cannot alone establish that a claim is true or a dataset fit for a particular purpose.[^R014][^R035][^R036]

The command should coordinate with the Defense Information Systems Agency (DISA) on infrastructure and secure operations, and with the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO) on data and AI evaluation.[^S001][^R025] It should work with Department of Defense records officials, component custodians, NARA, and other civilian custodians while respecting their existing responsibilities.[^R016][^R017][^R046] Civilian archives, universities, and research institutions could participate only through lawful agreements and applicable rights and access conditions. The proposed mission grants no general content-policing power, compulsory civilian collection authority, or control over civilian archives. These boundaries are central to the recommendation, not administrative details to be resolved after collection begins.

Fourth, the command should **demonstrate readiness**. It should conduct independent restoration exercises and task-relevant evaluations of data and models, including uncommon but consequential cases. Held-out evaluation material must be protected from training leakage so that test performance remains meaningful. Proposed reporting measures should include provenance coverage for prioritized collections, restoration outcomes, task-specific error and diversity changes, unresolved custody or access issues, and lifecycle costs—not just bytes collected. These are proposed measures, not claims about present performance. A successful checksum or restoration test proves only a limited property; evaluations must separately examine whether records and systems are suitable for their intended tasks.[^R014][^R016][^R017]

## Congressional Direction and Accountability

I recommend three concrete actions. First, Congress should authorize establishment of this narrow preservation-and-assurance mission, with named accountability, defined boundaries, and safeguards for civil liberties and civilian control. The proposal should not assume that existing general organizational law already authorizes this particular command. I recommend that the legislative request use the term Department of Defense unless Congress changes the law. The official 2024 edition of 10 U.S.C. §111(a) names that department; Executive Order 14347 permits secondary “Department of War” titles while retaining controlling statutory references.[^R019][^R021]

Second, Congress should require a costed, phased implementation plan that makes use of existing capabilities and identifies duplication before expansion. It should describe the proposed command’s relationships to DISA, CDAO, Department of Defense Chief Information Officer and component records officials, NARA, and other custodians; identify what should remain with each; and specify how retention, security, restoration, and evaluation would be funded.[^S001][^R016][^R017][^R025][^R046] This plan should compare establishing the command with strengthening existing offices and explain the expected added accountability. I do not recommend a dollar amount, staffing total, or savings figure without a reviewed basis. A new organization is not a substitute for demonstrating that it can perform the mission better and without avoidable duplication.

Third, Congress should require annual reporting and independent oversight by the Government Accountability Office or the Inspector General, with explicit safeguards for privacy, civil liberties, copyright, classification, and lawful access. Reporting should distinguish completed and independently checked results from planned work. It should disclose unresolved custody and access gaps, restoration exercise outcomes, task-specific evaluation findings, duplication identified, and lifecycle costs. Oversight should examine whether preservation and evaluation remain limited to the authorized mission, whether access controls and retention rules are followed, and whether the command’s activities respect civilian custodians and private rights. Existing archival standards and institutional responsibilities provide a foundation; the case for a focused military command is a policy judgment about accountable defense readiness, not a scientific finding that every other organizational model is inferior.[^R016][^R017][^R018][^R025][^R046]

These limits are practical safeguards. The proposed command should preserve only collections justified by defense mission needs and lawful access; it should not acquire a general mandate to monitor or judge civilian speech. It should leave archival custody and applicable records responsibilities where they belong, while making defense-critical material recoverable and its transformations traceable. It should use technical controls proportionate to risk, not assume that one label, detector, or provenance tool can classify all information reliably. Congress should expect the implementation plan to surface both costs and overlaps, and should retain oversight if the mission, partners, or data uses change.

## Closing

The decision before Congress is whether to establish an accountable, bounded defense mission that preserves recoverable information and tests whether it remains usable—not whether to declare all older data trustworthy or all synthetic data dangerous. I recommend establishing the Strategic Information Command, with clear civilian boundaries, cost discipline, and independent evaluation. Preserving original records cannot by itself guarantee good models or sound decisions, but losing the ability to inspect and recover them would narrow our options.[^R014][^R016][^R017] I thank the Committee for its attention and welcome your questions.
