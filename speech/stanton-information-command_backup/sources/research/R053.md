The increasing reliance on generative AI models is rapidly increasing the volume of synthetic data, with
projections suggesting that most available new data for training could be machine-generated by
20301. This shift to a mainly synthetic data presents a critical challenge: repeated training in synthetic
data leads to a phenomenon known as model collapse, where model performance degrades over
generations of training, eventually rendering the models ineffective. While the causes of model
collapse are increasingly understood, effective mitigation strategies remain scarce. We address this
challenge by leveraging a key insight: auto-regressive models tend to generate text sequences to
which they assign high conﬁdence. Based on this observation, we introduce Conﬁdence-Aware Loss
(CAL) functions, proposing Truncated Cross-Entropy (TCE) as a key instance. CAL mitigates model
collapse by down-weighing high-conﬁdence tokens during training, effectively ﬁltering out likely
machine-generated artifacts from the learning process. Our experiments demonstrate that models
trained with CAL not only learn effectively, but also exhibit signiﬁcantly increased resilience, tolerating
over 2.3 × more synthetic data before the onset of collapse. In addition, we provide an open-source
benchmark for collapse dynamics in mixed-data settings. Our results demonstrate that conﬁdenceaware training objectives can substantially delay collapse onset, offering a practical tool for model
robustness under synthetic-data exposure.
Generative models have become the foundation for modern AI applications in several modalities, including text, image, code, and audio.
Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT2, LLaMA3 and
Gemma4, as well as image generators DALL-E5 and Imagen6, all rely on
large datasets scraped from the Web. As these models are continuously
updated to reﬂect recent knowledge and linguistic patterns, the need for
ever larger and frequently refreshed training corpora has grown substantially. However, this demand is colliding with a shift in the data
landscape: synthetic content is increasingly populating the Internet,
contaminating the very datasets used for model training.
This shift raises fundamental concerns. Generative models trained on
outputs from earlier generations can degrade over time, a phenomenon
known as model collapse7–9. Even moderate levels of synthetic data (as little as
1%) have been shown to cause collapse, and scaling the model or the size of
the dataset fails to reliably prevent it10. Importantly, this is not a hypothetical
scenario: recent analysis of Alemohammad et al.11 on the LAION-5B
dataset, a cornerstone for many generative models, revealed measurable
contamination from synthetic sources. Following prior works7,8, we use the
term contamination or poisoning to describe the recursive use of synthetic
data in training, which leads to degradation in performance over

generations. As synthetic data becomes dominant, safeguarding generative
model stability becomes an urgent challenge.
Several approaches have been explored to mitigate model collapse.
Some studies combine synthetic and real data to slow degradation12; others
rely on post hoc supervision, such as SIMS13, using labeled synthetic data as
negative signal to improve generations or explore decoding strategies14. In
practice, however, distinguishing real from synthetic data remains a major
challenge. Watermarking methods15,16 have been proposed to help identify
model-generated content, but they are not widely adopted. Moreover, recent
critiques17 highlight that many prior studies simulate collapse in unrealistic
settings, such as using purely synthetic training data, limiting the applicability of their conclusions. Despite growing interest in this issue, the role of
the loss function, central to how models learn, has received little attention in
the context of model collapse.
Furthermore, model collapse is ampliﬁed by approximation errors and
ﬁnite sampling effects during training, which cause models to concentrate
probability mass on a shrinking subset of the true data distribution over
generations8,9.

their synthetic predictions (Fig. 1). This suggests that abnormally high

In brief, the contributions of ForTIFAI are as follows:
• We establish that a model’s conﬁdence, measured by sequence probability, serves as a strong signal for identifying self-generated text
within mixed datasets.
• We introduce CAL functions, speciﬁcally proposing TCE and identifying Focal Loss (FL) as key instances. By leveraging this signal to
mitigate model collapse, these approaches signiﬁcantly improve resilience, with TCE enabling models to tolerate more than 2.3 × greater
proportions of synthetic data without degradation.
• We propose a comprehensive evaluation framework for model collapse
that reﬂects realistic conditions, including the continual accumulation
of new data and the need to measure both retained and newly acquired
knowledge.

Datasets
We apply the recursive training setup illustrated in Fig. 2 on two datasets:
Wikitext-2-raw-v119 and a newly curated dataset we call Imagination
of Web.
Wikitext-2-raw-v1 is a language modeling dataset derived from highquality Wikipedia articles. Unlike its tokenized counterpart, it preserves raw
formatting—including punctuation, casing, and whitespace—making it
well-suited for evaluating a model’s ability to handle long-range dependencies and natural language structure.
The second dataset, Imagination of Web, is a composite dataset constructed from subsets of Wikitext, HellaSwag20, and GSM8k21. It is designed
to test the generalization of our loss functions across multiple tasks, such as
commonsense, math, and factual recall. This dataset is designed with the
goal of being more representative of the common data found on the web.

Model scale considerations
All experiments are conducted in a full-parameter training setting rather
than parameter-efﬁcient ﬁne-tuning (e.g., LoRA22). This design choice is
deliberate: model collapse is a distributional degeneration phenomenon that
emerges through iterative retraining, and partial parameter updates may not
faithfully reproduce the dynamics observed under full-model optimization.
Due to the substantial computational cost of repeated multi-stage fullparameter retraining, our largest evaluated Transformer models contain
1.5B parameters. Extending the framework to larger scales would require
signiﬁcantly greater computational budgets.
Intuitively, larger models better approximate the underlying data distribution, reducing early-stage over-ampliﬁcation of high-conﬁdence
tokens. We therefore expect the qualitative behavior of CAL to persist at
larger scales, though comprehensive large-scale validation remains important future work. Further details are provided in the Supplementary
Information section “Inevitable Model Collapse”.
Knowledge retention test
To comprehensively assess the impact of our loss functions on model
performance and resilience to model collapse, we perform evaluations on a
mix of standard and custom benchmarks. These benchmarks test various
capabilities, including factual recall, linguistic coherence, commonsense,
and mathematical reasoning. In addition to widely used benchmarks like
BLiMP, Hellaswag, and GSM8K, we utilize a question-answering benchmark—Knowledge Retention test (KR-test)23 to measure how well models
retain training-set facts.
Validation perplexity is a common metric for evaluating LLMs, but it
primarily reﬂects predictive ﬂuency; how well a model guesses the next
token in unseen text. While useful for assessing grammar and style, it does
not quantify factual knowledge retention. Moreover, low perplexity does not
guarantee meaningful or human-like output, as humans do not always select
the most likely next word.
To address this gap, we use the Knowledge Retention Test (KR-test)23,
which measures factual retention from the training data rather than general
ﬂuency.

three reasoning and language understanding benchmarks. These evaluations assess whether our loss functions preserve or enhance generalization
and linguistic structure, both of which are vulnerable under recursive
training regimes.

capability, we use three standard benchmarks: GSM8K 21, Hellaswag 20, and
BLiMP 24.

• GSM8K measures mathematical reasoning by testing the model’s
ability to solve grade-school level word problems. Success on this
benchmark reﬂects the model’s ability to perform multi-step arithmetic
reasoning.

Time to failure
We deﬁne time to failure as the number of re-training tokens it takes a model
in the context of this framework to degrade signiﬁcantly compared to the
initial model. Speciﬁcally, we analyze the performance of the model on the
ﬁrst partition of the dataset (KR-test consisting only of questions from M0),
which is re-generated at every stage. Moreover, since the KR-test on average
has a baseline of 50% and a maximum of 100% is achievable, we deﬁne
failure as performance dropping below 75%, which is exactly half the
maximum gain one can expect from training the model. As shown in Fig. 5,
CE fails between the ﬁrst and second stages, while both CAL instances
extend time to failure by over 2.3 × . These results suggest CAL is effective at
sustaining meaningful retention under compounding self-generation.

Across all stages, every tested γ for both TCE and FL improves upon
CE, indicating that CAL methods are not highly sensitive to precise
hyperparameter selection. However, a consistent pattern emerges: moderate
values of γ provide the most favorable trade-off between collapse mitigation
and preservation of general capabilities.
For TCE, values in the range γ ∈ [0.85, 0.9] repeatedly achieve top or
near-top performance. For focal loss, γ = 2 provides the most stable
improvements across benchmarks, consistent with prior ﬁndings26, while
larger values can overly suppress gradients and degrade later-stage
performance.
Intuitively, small γ in TCE aggressively truncates high-conﬁdence
tokens, potentially removing informative signal. Similarly, large γ in FL
excessively re-weights gradients. Moderate values selectively suppress the
extreme high-conﬁdence tail, where recursive collapse manifests, without
destabilizing optimization.
In practice, we recommend inspecting conﬁdence histograms and
selecting γ to trim only the extreme high-conﬁdence region while retaining
the bulk of the distribution. Our experiments suggest γ ≈ 0.85-0.9 for TCE
and γ ≈ 2 for FL as robust starting points. A deeper theoretical characterization of optimal γ selection across domains remains an important direction for future work.

Discussion
We have demonstrated that a CAL function, namely TCE, and FL, provide a
simple and powerful mitigation strategy against model collapse. Grounded
in a mathematical framework linking overconﬁdence to distributional drift,
CAL extends a model’s operational lifetime by over 2.3 × compared to
standard CE. Our benchmarks conﬁrm that this approach not only delays
collapse but also improves knowledge retention and generalization in
environments with accumulating synthetic data.
Crucially, the principle of ﬁltering overconﬁdent outputs is not
domain-speciﬁc. We veriﬁed its efﬁcacy in diverse generative systems,
including Gaussian Mixture Models and Variational Autoencoders, as
shown in the Supplementary Information. As the line between human
and machine-generated data continues to blur, such robust, lowoverhead training methods will be essential for building stable and
trustworthy AI.
Beyond language modeling, Fig. S2 and Algorithm S1 show that
applying the truncation strategy to Gaussian Mixture Models effectively
delays distributional collapse, while Fig. S3 demonstrates that a truncated
VAE maintains image ﬁdelity across recursive self-generation stages. These
experiments conﬁrm that the proposed CAL generalizes to a wide range of
generative architectures.
We approached model collapse from a training perspective and proposed a class of loss functions that mitigate collapse effectively without
sacriﬁcing performance. By reweighting token probabilities to favor
underrepresented or underestimated outputs, these losses retain the simplicity of CE while offering greater resilience during recursive training.
Looking ahead, several research avenues merit further exploration:
• Broader model classes: Model collapse has been extensively studied in
image generation domains. It remains an open question whether
similar loss-based strategies can be adapted to diffusion models27 and

Methods
Problem deﬁnition
Model Collapse is the gradual degradation that occurs when a model’s own
outputs pollute the training data of its successors. Each new model then
learns from these possibly ﬂawed examples, causing a drift from the true
data distribution7.
The fundamental cause of collapse is the discrepancy between the true
data distribution, Pdata, and the model’s learned approximation of it, Pθ.
Because any realizable model has ﬁnite capacity and is trained on ﬁnite data,
its outputs will contain statistical artifacts not present in the true distribution. Compounding this issue, the decoding process itself introduces a
further distributional shift. Common decoding algorithms (e.g., greedy
search, beam search) are designed to favor high-probability sequences
rather than sampling purely from the model’s learned distribution. This bias
means that even if a model were a perfect representation of the data (Pθ =
Pdata), the generated text would over-represent the modes of the
distribution.
When these skewed outputs are used to train a successor model, the
imperfections are learned and ampliﬁed. This iterative process leads to a
progressive distributional drift, causing a catastrophic loss of information,
particularly in the tails of the distribution, ultimately making the model
unusable.

This setup also follows the conditions mentioned in 17:
1. Increasing pre-training data: Unlike previous studies with ﬁxed
dataset sizes, our setup continuously increases the pre-training
data at each generation. This growth mirrors real-world trends
among state-of-the-art models; for example, LLaMA 1, 2, and 3
were trained on 1.4, 2, and 15 trillion tokens, respectively.
Although dataset growth has eventual limits, these bounds remain
extremely high (approximately one quadrillion tokens)30, far
exceeding current dataset sizes.
2. Synthetic data accumulation alongside real data: Another distinctive
feature of our framework is its simultaneous training on both real and
synthetic data. Unlike previous work, we do not discount the effect of
real unseen data on model collapse. We argue that the real unseen data
is the main reason behind training the new generation of models in the
ﬁrst place.
3. Decreasing proportion of real data: A primary concern highlighted in
recent literature is the accelerating generation rate of synthetic data and
its impact on future generative models. Our experimental setup realistically emulates this crucial aspect (e.g., in our setup with 6 stages, we
have 100%, 50%, 33%, 25%, 20%, and 15% real data at each stage,
respectively).

Both Focal Loss (FL) and TCE aim to suppress the dominance of
high-conﬁdence predictions; however, they differ fundamentally in
how they modulate the training signal. Focal Loss applies a smooth
multiplicative reweighting of the gradient across the entire conﬁdence
spectrum, scaling each token’s contribution by (1−p)γ, where p is the
predicted probability of the ground-truth token. Consequently, even
highly conﬁdent predictions continue to receive non-zero gradient
updates, albeit attenuated. In contrast, TCE implements a hard truncation: tokens whose conﬁdence exceeds a threshold are completely
masked from gradient updates. Under the Gaussian model, this is
equivalent to conditioning exclusively on the tail region ∣Xt∣≥aσt,
yielding an explicit variance ampliﬁcation factor η(a) > 1.

Model collapse evaluation framework
To realistically assess the impact of Model Collapse, we adopt an experimental framework inspired by ref. 12. Initially, web data was predominantly
human-generated and often curated for quality. Over time, however,
machine-generated content, often indistinguishable from human-written
text, has increasingly supplemented online data. Our framework simulates a
realistic model collapse process by progressively adding synthetic data to the
training set. We evaluate the methods on math, reasoning, and knowledge
recall benchmark performance. Moreover, we analyze the number of synthetic training iterations it takes for the model to lose performance below a
certain threshold, which we call the time to failure.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the experiment proceeds in multiple stages.
At each stage, the dataset simulates a large-scale pretraining corpus,
composed of both authentic and self-generated data. At every step, we
regenerate the previous stage’s dataset using the latest model and concatenate the dataset with a ﬁxed amount of new human-written text.
