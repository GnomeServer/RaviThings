## Official article text

By Tim D. Godbee, DISA Public Affairs

In his closing keynote address at the [AFCEA International](https://www.linkedin.com/company/afcea-international?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) TechNet Augusta 2026 conference on Aug. 20, [US Army](https://www.linkedin.com/company/us-army?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Lt. Gen. Paul T. Stanton, [United States Department of War](https://www.linkedin.com/company/deptofwar?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Cyber Defense Command commander and [Defense Information Systems Agency](https://www.linkedin.com/company/dowdisa?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) director, delivered a blunt capstone message to military and industry partners: the joint force must stop treating its networks as static IT infrastructure and start commanding them as premier strategic weapons systems.

Speaking to an audience of military leaders, cyber operators and commercial technology developers at the home of the Army Cyber Center of Excellence, Stanton warned that legacy administrative frameworks and bespoke architectures are actively compounding operational risk.
> “The timelines for competition and conflict have compressed exponentially,” Stanton said. “We are facing an environment where threat detection, vulnerability exploitation and active network penetration occur in minutes, not months.”

To outmaneuver near-peer adversaries in this compressed digital battlespace, Stanton laid out an aggressive vision centered on unified data standards, agile coalition integration and a ruthless cultural shift in how the military maintains its digital high ground.

### Redefining combined arms in the AI era

Stanton opened by challenging the audience to look beyond individual technological buzzwords like artificial intelligence, large language models and agentic capabilities, and instead focus on how they must be integrated to transform maneuver warfare.

He pointed to a historical parallel to illustrate his point. When German military strategists revolutionized modern combined arms doctrine in the 20th century, they did not invent the tank, the airplane or the machine gun.

“They put it together and redefined combined arms warfare,” Stanton explained. “What I ask you to think about is: how do we put together large language models, frontier analysis and agentic capabilities at speed, scope and scale in order to get the right data to the right place at the right time?”

Stanton noted that frontier AI models can now discover network vulnerabilities exponentially faster than human analysts. Securing the Department of War Information Network, or DOWIN, under this relentless pressure, he argued, requires a baseline of absolute standardization.

### Normalizing the 'caliber' of joint data

To ground the technical complexities of network architecture in terms familiar to the combat soldiers in the room, Stanton compared data flows to infantry logistics.
> “When you fight in the infantry, we have normalized the caliber of our ammunition so that different weapon systems can fire the same round,” Stanton said. “Hopefully, all you nerds in the room are making the quick analogy to data.”

Stanton painted a picture of the battlefield logistics chain: if a supply truck is loaded with 30 different incompatible calibers of ammunition, the logistics line clogs and the front lines go dry.

“If our data looks completely different, we have different calibers of ammunition,” he warned. “And we are trying to load it onto our weapons systems, and we are trying to move it to the point where it needs to be, and we are experiencing congestion. We cannot afford that. Speed matters.”

### Building with "Lego blocks"

The solution to this data bottleneck, according to Stanton, is a modular, standardized architecture built like commercial Lego bricks.

“I use the analogy of Lego blocks all the time because I think it holds,” he said. “You need composability, you need extensibility, but you need a degree of commonality. When you dump your Legos out onto the floor, you can connect them together in interesting ways because there is a standardized, common interface.”

This Lego-like design is the core of the military’s Next-Generation Command and Control, or NextGen C2 initiative. Stanton explained that when a standardized Army corps or division headquarters deploys to U.S. Pacific Command, its networks must "snap in" instantly. If they deploy to U.S. Central Command, they must snap in just as seamlessly.

This commonality becomes even more critical when integrating international allies, who often arrive with their own unique digital systems.

“That coalition is going to bring their own box of Legos,” Stanton joked. “Those Legos may be Duplo blocks. They may not fit exactly.”

As a result, Stanton charged DISA with aggressively defining the gateway architectures that translate and secure foreign data feeds, turning coalition diversity into a plug-and-play asymmetric advantage.

### Banning the 'POAM' and restoring command maintenance

The most passionate moment of Stanton's keynote focused on a critical shift in military cyber culture. The DISA director declared that the network command must stop tolerating persistent, unpatched vulnerabilities hidden behind administrative paperwork.

Specifically, Stanton targeted the ubiquitous Plan of Action and Milestones, or POAMs — an administrative tool often used to delay necessary network fixes.
> “POAM is a bad word inside of DISA and DCDC,” Stanton declared. “Legitimately, you can’t say it to me. It’s a trigger word. If you say POAM on the operations center floor, both the individual who said it and I drop to the floor to do pushups.”

True to his word, Stanton paused his address to drop and perform five pushups on the stage, drawing roaring applause from the crowd.

Stanton argued that relying on POAMs is "kicking the can down the road" while incurring compounding operational risk. He compared the practice to "Circle-Xing" a military vehicle — a process where a commander signs off on a damaged piece of equipment to keep it moving in combat.

“When you Circle-X a Bradley Fighting Vehicle, behind the scenes, the part is on order, the mechanics are in a huddle and the moment it is out of contact, it goes back into maintenance,” Stanton said. “We have been Circle-Xing the network into perpetuity without ever going back to actually do the maintenance, hiding underneath the umbrella of a POAM. That doesn't work.”

To drive home the danger of ignoring persistent network warnings, Stanton recalled an experience at the Amsterdam airport. A door to the flight line was opened, triggering a deafening emergency alarm. Instead of reacting, the travelers in the terminal sat entirely unfazed.

“What did we do? Sat there in a drone, doing absolutely nothing,” Stanton said. He warned that the joint force cannot afford "alert fatigue" when defending critical warfighting networks.

Instead of administrative delays, Stanton called for a strict "deferred maintenance" model, forcing commanders to actively manage and offset the real-time operational risks of unpatched systems.

### A frontline call to action

Stanton closed by challenging both the commercial tech sector and the military's frontline cyber operators to work with combat urgency.

To industry, he asked for a focus on practical, edge-based AI solutions rather than generic tools. "Let's start working toward the actually operationally focused use cases, because understanding the use case drives the whole rest of the chain."

To military cyber forces, his message was clear: cyber is a maneuver domain and the networks are strategic weapons.
> “You are expected to command and maneuver your networks with the exact same aggression, initiative and intent demanded on any physical battlefield,” Stanton concluded. “Our nation needs you. Let's get after this. Let's go.”
