## Official article text

By Aiyana S. Wangler, DISA Public Affairs

As artificial intelligence rapidly reshapes the cybersecurity landscape, [US Army](https://www.linkedin.com/company/us-army?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Lt. Gen. Paul T. Stanton urged IT and cyber professionals to double down on foundational technical skills and abandon outdated maintenance practices during his Aug. 25 keynote at the 2026 Department of the Air Force Information Technology and Cyberpower Conference in Montgomery, Ala.

Speaking near Maxwell Air Force Base, a hub for military education, the [United States Department of War](https://www.linkedin.com/company/deptofwar?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Cyber Defense Command commander and Defense Information Systems Agency director emphasized that mastering the basics is a prerequisite for surviving the modern cyber battlefield.
> "If you don't understand the basics, the foundations, the fundamentals, networking, coding, cyber defense, hunting operations, then you cannot keep pace with the incredible change occurring in our warfighting domain," Stanton said.

The need for deep expertise

Stanton warned that the speed, scope and scale of modern cyber threats mean the U.S. military cannot simply stumble into solutions involving large language models and agentic-based AI. Personnel must actively study the changing environment.

He cautioned against generalizing IT roles, stressing that the department needs deep specialization to drive real innovation.

"Saying that you are a technologist in the current cyberspace warfighting domain is like saying that you're a doctor," Stanton explained. "Do you want your podiatrist performing neurosurgery? They're both doctors. Probably not, is my assumption. But folks that understand the depth of networking, folks that understand the depth of coding–pick your language–…have to come together to innovate and solve problems.”

A new era of maintenance

To protect these complex, data-driven systems, Stanton is enforcing a radical shift in how the department handles network vulnerabilities.

Stanton argues POAMs have historically been used to defer cybersecurity maintenance indefinitely under the guise of prioritizing current missions. He instead replaced this outdated practice with a strict "deferred maintenance program."

Stanton likened a network to an aircraft or a tank. While maintenance can be temporarily delayed to execute an immediate kinetic mission, the network must be patched the moment the operation concludes.

"Our adversary is going to attack that vulnerable system," Stanton said. "If the network is no longer available, neither are any of our weapons systems that depend on data."

Industry teammates and continuous innovation

Because the Department of War Information Network is largely an amalgamation of commercial products, Stanton stressed that true innovation requires viewing industry providers as integral teammates rather than just contractors.

The technical depth required to understand rapid technological shifts often originate in the private sector. Stanton challenged his workforce to stay tuned into industry research and development to seamlessly integrate new ideas into the military's current solutions.
> "Organizations with whom we establish contracts are not contractors; they're teammates," said Stanton. "We do it together. We do it as part of a joint force. We do it as part of a coalition. We do it in defense of the United States of America."

When new technologies are introduced, Stanton advised that the cyber force must return to the "proverbial range" to requalify, ensuring that learning never stops.

"There’s no doctrine or manual, I’m just going to tell you how to do it," Stanton concluded. "It rides on the broad shoulders of the individuals in the room to own the responsibility, be self-directed learners to stay in touch with the industry developments ... and drive our forces into the future."
