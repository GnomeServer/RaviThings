The U.S. Department of War developed its cyber warfare framework during an era in which
human decision-making could generally keep pace with technological change and adversarial
activity. Today, advances in frontier artificial intelligence (AI) and autonomous cyber capabilities
are transforming the need for cyber operational speed, scope, and scale. As traditional timelines
for detection, decision-making, and response continue to compress, preserving mission assurance
will require more than technological innovation alone. As Commander of the DOW Cyber Defense
Command (DCDC) and Director of the Defense Information Systems Agency (DISA), Army Lt.
Gen. Paul T. Stanton is leading his organizations to campaign accordingly, setting conditions to
effectively engage the enemy through defense. Stanton charges the department with an imperative
to continue strengthening and modernizing our cyber architecture, accounting for full-scope
resourcing and operationalizing our partnerships with industry.

Keywords: cyber defense, mission assurance, artificial intelligence, DOTmLPF-P, cyber resilience, public-
private partnerships, digital infrastructure

THE CHANGING CHARACTER OF CYBER WARFARE
As The Cyber Defense Review marks another milestone in its contribution to the study and practice of
cybersecurity, it is worth reflecting on how much has changed and what those changes reveal about
the future of the cyber warfighting domain. Over the past decade, the department has made significant
progress in cyber warfare planning, capabilities, and operations. Today, every major national security
mission depends upon digital infrastructure, data, software, communications networks, and the
organizations that operate them.
  In 2018, U.S. Cyber Command’s strategy of persistent engagement through a professional force set
the stage for cyber operations (Nakasone 2019). The approach has matured into cyber campaigning
that remains a sound framework (U.S. Cyber Command PAO 2022; Fischerkeller, Goldman, and
Harknett 2025). Building upon this solid foundation, investments in cybersecurity modernization,
cloud adoption, zero-trust architecture, cyber workforce development, and enterprise visibility have
produced meaningful improvements. These aligned efforts have enhanced our ability to identify risks,
understand our networks, and transition our operational mindset from reactive incident response to
active, synchronized defense against sophisticated adversaries. However, we must do more.

   The DOW Information Network (DOWIN) is a strategic weapon system that underpins global
command and control, intelligence, logistics, and military operations. In support of its pivotal role, the
department has made significant progress in strengthening its security and resilience. However, many
of the systems governing cybersecurity today were established when technological change occurred at
a comparatively slower pace. Though they continue to serve important functions, preserving mission
assurance now and into the future will require transforming legacy structures to adapt to the speed,
scope, and scale of tomorrow’s fight. Throughout this transition, we must continue to focus on what
matters: effective mission operations calibrated against enemy intent and capability. Our focus must
remain on campaigning to deliver exactly what our warfighting commanders require to execute and
sustain their missions at the speed of relevance.
  The challenge of operating at speed extends far beyond technology. It demands structural trans-
formation toward modern and agile cyber architecture, sustained, full-scope enterprise resourcing,
and seamlessly integrated partnerships with the commercial sector. The future of cyber campaign-
ing will not be determined by which organizations possess the most advanced technologies, but by
which organizations can most effectively convert awareness into action, coordinate across complex
ecosystems, and sustain critical missions under increasingly dynamic conditions.


SHARED RISK EMERGES
We have crossed a strategic threshold. The emergence of frontier AI models, increasingly autonomous
cyber systems, and highly scalable digital technologies is accelerating the evolution of cyber warfare.
Due to global reliance on cyber systems, frontier AI models introduce a disruptive surge to cyber
operations. We are now engaged in a fierce capability competition that will dictate the future char-
acter of warfare, intelligence, and national security. Frontier AI models and autonomous systems
are compressing timelines across vulnerability discovery, software analysis, exploit development,
operational planning, and influence operations—creating a narrow margin between discovery and
exploitation. As algorithms become exponentially more capable, traditional buffer zones for reactions
are evaporating, requiring automated, near-instantaneous action to outmaneuver adversaries.
  This challenge means cyber defenders must access information and rapidly translate it to awareness
within a synchronized, operational-level campaign before adversaries can achieve effects. Doing so
effectively requires the institutions responsible for defending national security missions to adapt
quickly. The imperative for strategic adaptation becomes clear when considering what is at stake. The
Joint Force operates with unprecedented connectedness because the department’s digital architecture
enables the synchronization of global command and control, distribution of real-time intelligence,
coordination of complex logistical and sustainment networks, and the projection of strategic deter-
rence. Our modern operational advantages—speed of decision, synchronized maneuver, and unified
command—depend upon the availability and absolute integrity of the DOWIN.
  Compounding this complexity, our digital architecture is not a closed system. Joint operations rely
on commercial cloud services, civilian telecommunications, and critical infrastructure owned and
operated entirely outside the DOW. Mission assurance is therefore inextricably tied to the resilience of
the commercial sector. Our adversaries clearly understand this asymmetric vulnerability. They system-
atically target these commercial providers precisely because their networks support our warfighting

ADAPTATION AS A STRATEGIC IMPERATIVE
While the enduring principles that underpin military effectiveness remain constant, the application of
those principles must evolve to match a shifting operational environment. Today, frontier AI models
are driving significant and rapid evolution, placing an unprecedented premium on our institutional
capacity to adapt. This adaptation requires a parallel evolution of our technical architecture, our
institutional frameworks, and our operational partnerships. This demands we align our doctrine,
command structures, training systems, and administrative policies with the speed of modern conflict.
   We must systematically reassess how we measure risk, how we leverage authorities, how decentral-
ized forces coordinate, and how mission priorities dictate defensive actions. Mission owners, network
defenders, critical infrastructure operators, and commercial partners will shape operational outcomes
through their collective decisions. Preserving mission assurance requires agile digital architecture
operated by fully resourced institutions that partner with industry to defend our nation.

Designing an Architecture for Agility

To outpace our adversaries in an environment defined by automated speed, we cannot simply accu-
mulate software or layer new tools on top of fragile systems. We must move faster by designing and
implementing the resilient environment we need. While we are making progress, we must accelerate
our transition by systematically eliminating technical debt, replacing obsolete infrastructure and es-
tablishing a secure, standardized enterprise baseline across the force. This enduring digital foundation
is the prerequisite for processing data at the speed of conflict and enabling seamless command and
control across every warfighting domain.
  Well-designed and modernized architecture serves as the critical enabler to achieve operational
velocity. It establishes the necessary structural clarity and real-time operational context across our vast,
globally distributed enterprise. The architectural framework empowers decentralized commanders to
make decisions that are locally risk-informed, yet globally risk-bounded. This operational standard
is enforced through a zero-trust architecture that integrates federated identity, credential, and ac-
cess management with automated, context-aware policy enforcement. By enforcing cryptographic
boundaries and dynamic network segmentation at the tactical edge, we can isolate and contain
localized compromises before they propagate across our global transport layers. Automating this

Full DOTmLPF-P Resourcing

Effectively running a modernized architecture and operationally controlling that architecture requires
comprehensive institutional resourcing. We cannot move at increased and sustained speed, scope,
and scale with solutions that only address parts of the problem. We must align our joint planning
using the DOTmLPF-P doctrine, organization, training, materiel, leadership and education, personnel,
facilities, and policy framework to structurally synchronize our capabilities (Joint Chiefs of Staff 2026).
  Starting with personnel, cyber warriors must profoundly understand the mission they support, the
dependencies that enable those missions and the operational consequences of cyber risk. They must
be prepared to operate tactically alongside highly capable and automated systems while retaining
responsibility for strategic and operational consequences. Ultimately, our decisive advantage in
cyberspace will not be the algorithms we deploy, but the ingenuity, adaptability and operational
mindset of the professionals who wield them.
  Developing our professionals will require processes that can keep pace with updated tactics, tech-
niques, and procedures. We need trained, accountable personnel who follow standardized doctrine
that ensures disciplined execution to run architecture with the full power of AI capabilities. We cannot
coordinate action without a shared understanding of how we will execute. Designing and modernizing
our digital environment is useless if we fail to qualify the professionals who wield it. Adhering to this
comprehensive planning construct provides the essential blueprint for full-spectrum force resourcing,
moving us beyond a compliance-driven model to a culture of rigorous, periodic qualification on our
cyber systems. We must build readiness.
  This readiness is crucial when confronting the staggering speed of frontier AI, which collapses the
window for vulnerability discovery to active exploit from months to minutes. In this high-velocity
threat environment, passive security updates and check-the-box patching are obsolete. We must
enforce a rigorous standard of action plus validation, confirming that every configuration change
and security remediation actually closes the vulnerability without disrupting our broader operational
capabilities. Sustaining mission-critical operations under these contested conditions requires us to view
cyber defense not as traditional technology procurement, but as a core warfighting capability fully
aligned with the long-term training, testing, and readiness requirements embedded in the DOTMLPF-P
framework, including our industry partners.

Operationalizing Partnerships with Industry

We can no longer afford to assess the shared risk environment through an isolated lens. The digital
ecosystem that supports national security missions extends beyond the boundaries of the DOW. This
reality reflects both the innovation and scale that commercial industry brings to national defense
and the deeply interconnected nature of modern operations. Consequently, the resilience of national
security missions depends on the resilience of the broader ecosystem that enables military power.
  Interdependence with industry is not new, but the pace of cyber competition and contention is
changing its implications. Transactional information sharing between government and industry
remains valuable but is no longer sufficient. As timelines compress, it is the ability to act collectively
on shared information that matters. Government, industry and critical infrastructure operators must
develop shared understanding, assess mission consequences and coordinate defensive actions quickly
enough to influence outcomes. The objective is not simply greater awareness—it is reducing the time
required to move from awareness to coordinated response.
   Operational integration at speed requires trusted relationships, shared planning, routine exercises
and clearly understood responsibilities well before a crisis occurs. Organizations that attempt to
establish those relationships during an incident are unlikely to operate at the speed future conditions
will require. The goal is to move beyond the transactional, bilateral nature of service-level contracts and
programmatic task orders toward deep, operationalized teamwork. The department and its industry
partners can best achieve integration through coordinated incident response, rapid vulnerability
mitigation and proactive defenses. Cyber defense in the frontier AI era relies on integrated teammates
ready to campaign together with synchronized defensive actions against common adversaries to
achieve a level of collective resilience that contracts alone can never guarantee.

CONCLUSION
The true measure of our future success will not be found in isolated pockets of excellence, but in
our capacity to proactively campaign for rapid, collective action. In a domain where milliseconds
matter, the artificial boundaries between military organizations, government agencies and commercial
partners must give way to a seamless, integrated defense. When we align our mission purpose, share
our visibility, and synchronize our responses, we transform our interconnectedness from a potential
vulnerability into our greatest asymmetric advantage. It is this unified effort—orchestrated with
unprecedented efficiency and mutual trust—that will allow us to outpace any adversary.
  Defending the DOWIN requires more than cutting-edge technology—it demands an operational
mindset rooted in resilient architecture, fully resourced institutions and commercial teamwork to
operate with synchronization. Ultimately, the decisive advantage in the future of cyberspace will
not belong to the organization that possesses the most advanced algorithms or the most expansive
networks. It will belong to the organization that is ready and can adapt the fastest to empower its
cyber professionals to execute at the speed, scope, and scale of the fight. For the DOW, adapting to this
new reality is not just an administrative goal—it is a strategic and operational imperative. If we are to
secure the systems that enable military power and preserve our ability to fight and win in a contested
environment, we must build an enterprise designed not just to withstand modern cyber conflict, but
to dictate it.
