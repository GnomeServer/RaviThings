## Official article text

By Marco A. Villasana Jr., DISA Public Affairs

In testimony before the Senate Armed Services Subcommittee on Cybersecurity, [US Army](https://www.linkedin.com/company/us-army?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Lt. Gen. Paul T. Stanton outlined a sweeping transformation in how the military builds, operates and defends its global information network, telling a Senate panel on March 24, that the network must be treated as a "critical weapons system."

Stanton, who serves as both the [United States Department of War](https://www.linkedin.com/company/deptofwar?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-mention) Cyber Defense Command commander and the Defense Information Systems Agency director, stressed that the digital domain is a contested battlefield, adding that a "fundamental shift" is underway to ensure U.S. forces maintain a decision advantage over their adversaries.
> "In the face of determined and capable adversaries across the globe, the joint force’s success on the battlefield comes down to one core task: getting the right data to the right place at the right time to make a better and faster decision than the enemy," Stanton said.

The general's testimony centered on four key areas: delivering decisive capabilities to warfighters, building an architecture that is "secure by design," shifting to a proactive operational mindset and revolutionizing personnel readiness.

A cornerstone of the new strategy is modern and defensible architecture built on the principle of Zero Trust. This "never trust, always verify" model continuously validates every user and device on the network, a critical defense against modern cyberattacks.

"This is the essential model that enables the agile, data-centric Mission Partner Environment, allowing us to move beyond the legacy approach of building isolated hardware-defined networks for each coalition," Stanton explained.

To deliver capabilities more effectively, DISA is collapsing disparate, single-purpose networks into a standardized and unified system. Through efforts like Mission Network as a Service, the agency is changing how warfighters access critical data.

"Instead of building mission networks from scratch — a process that can take months — commanders can incorporate combat power according to mission requirements," Stanton said. This approach extends to coalition partners through the Coalition Information Environment, which aims to break down technical barriers and improve interoperability with allies.

This transformation in technology is matched by a new operational approach. Stanton described a move away from "reactive network defense" toward orchestrating a continuous, proactive campaign against adversaries. He noted that the recent elevation of DCDC to a sub-unified command was a foundational step in this evolution.

"We are moving beyond chasing individual alerts to campaigning against systemic vulnerabilities and coordinated threat actors," Stanton testified. He highlighted that the synergy created by his dual-hatted leadership "seamlessly fuses DCDC’s operational ability to maneuver and defend the network with DISA’s deep technical knowledge of the underlying infrastructure."

Ultimately, Stanton emphasized that people are the bedrock of the nation's cyber defense.
> "Technology provides the tools, but it is trained and accountable people who win battles, which is why readiness is a non-negotiable, number one priority of both the command and the agency," he stated.

This "readiness revolution" focuses on acquiring elite talent, delivering advanced training, and enforcing a culture of accountability to professionalize the cyber workforce.

In closing, Stanton underscored the urgency and continuous nature of the mission, linking the security of the Department of War Information Network directly to the security of the nation.

"At the forefront of this constant, daily fight are the dedicated men and women of DCDC and DISA," Stanton concluded. "They are our most critical asset."
