## Study design

This paper studies iterative estimation where every training round receives a newly collected batch of real data and a synthetic batch sampled from the preceding model. It specifically treats real/synthetic source as known, sample sizes as fixed by round, and tests weights separately from sample counts. It is therefore neither the fixed-original-data setup of Bertrand et al. (R074) nor an unqualified study of all language-model pretraining. Its motivating scenario includes loss of access to historical raw data or informative reusable summaries; it does not show that a preservation archive is unnecessary or substitute for data collection (Introduction, §§1–2.2).

The weight `w` is assigned to the real-data estimator and `1-w` to synthetic data. For Gaussian estimation, the weighted mean and covariance are fitted separately with squared-error criteria on each batch (Eq. 1–2, §3.1). The generalized linear model extension is analyzed asymptotically; examples include linear, logistic, and Poisson regression (§3.2). A nonparametric distribution-estimation extension updates empirical cumulative distribution functions and evaluates error using the Cramér–von Mises criterion (§4).

## Results and conditions

- **Gaussian mean:** With `k=n/m` the ratio of real to synthetic batch sizes, the optimal constant weight on real data is `w* = (sqrt(k²+4k)-k)/2` (Theorem 1, §3.1). Only when the batches are equal (`n=m`, `k=1`) does this equal the reciprocal golden ratio. The result is for the stated Gaussian mean-estimation objective and constant-in-time weighting, not a universal language-model training ratio. The paper separately notes that horizon-dependent weights can change the rate (§3.1, Remark 3).
- **Gaussian covariance:** Under its stated Gaussian setting, the paper analyzes covariance-estimation error, including a large-sample regime where its optimal weight has the same formula (Corollary 1, §3.1). This is a statistical estimation result, not a model-wide quality/diversity certificate.
- **Generalized linear models:** Under explicit assumptions on sub-Gaussian covariates, smoothness, finite moments, identifiable curvature at the true parameter, and local third-derivative control, Theorem 2 gives a recursion for scaled asymptotic parameter-estimation risk. In that asymptotic regime, constant-in-time risk is minimized by the same weight formula for a fixed sample-size ratio `k`; Corollary 2 compares this with naive weighting (Theorem 2 and Corollary 2, §3.2). The conditions and GLM class constrain the result; it is not a theorem about neural language models.
- **Nonparametric CDF:** Under a continuous target density, fixed batch sizes, and a Cramér–von Mises loss, Theorem 3 gives limiting error and an optimal weight (Eq. 10 and Theorem 3, §4). At zero real weight, this bounded metric approaches 1/6, rather than diverging; the paper's operational definition of collapse and the metric therefore matter. A finite value under this one metric is not evidence that diversity, tail coverage, or factual accuracy is preserved.
- **Experiments:** Simulations cover Gaussian estimation, regression models, and CDF estimation. The real-data illustration uses a logistic regression on the Adult dataset, treats a model fit on the full data as a pseudo-ground-truth parameter, fixes real and synthetic batch sizes at 500 each, and compares weights over 100 rounds (§5.2). These are statistical and tabular tasks, not foundation-model experiments.

## Limitations and interpretation

The framework depends on distinguishing real from synthetic samples; §6 explicitly names imperfect source information/provenance as a practical limitation. Its fresh-data setting is analytically different from retaining original records: each round uses a new real batch, while this paper's design excludes accumulation of all past real observations. A constant optimal weight does not optimize every alternative, including adaptive/horizon-dependent schemes. The golden-ratio value is restricted to equal-sized real/synthetic batches within the analyzed weighting objective. These results cannot specify a universal fraction or threshold for LLMs, different modalities, or broader training pipelines. Provenance records alone do not establish that an observation is true or suitable for training.
